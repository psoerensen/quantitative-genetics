Course map

Population genetics, quantitative genetics and breeding decisions

Choose an entry point

The practicals connect three questions: how genetic variation is described, how genetic merit is predicted, and how breeding decisions change a population. PG denotes a population-genetics practical and QG a quantitative-genetics practical; each course has its own numbering.

Start with basic genetics, means and variances, and elementary R vectors, data frames and plots. Matrix notation becomes useful for genetic evaluation and selection indices. Each practical includes explanation, runnable examples, interpretation questions and suggested solutions.

Arrows show useful conceptual connections, not compulsory software dependencies. You can teach the short routes below without running every preceding analysis.

Population-genetics sequence

These eight practicals use base R and hypothetical inputs. They require no biological downloads or additional R packages.

Practical Main question Useful preparation
PG 1: Understanding genotype data What does an allele dosage encode, and what does it omit? Genotypes, alleles and basic R
PG 2: Allele frequencies and HWE How do observed counts differ from random-union expectations? PG 1
PG 3: Structure, mating and inbreeding Can a heterozygosity deficit identify its cause? PG 2; conditional probability
PG 4: Measuring genetic diversity Which diversity measure matches the data and sampling model? PG 1–3
PG 5: Mutation, migration and LD How do processes change allele and haplotype frequencies? PG 2; phased haplotypes from PG 1
PG 6: Genetic drift Why do finite populations depart from deterministic expectations? PG 2 and PG 4; binomial sampling
PG 7: Selection, fitness and dominance How do genotype fitness and sampling change allele trajectories? PG 2 and PG 6
PG 8: Testing HWE proportions What do approximate and exact tests establish? PG 2–3; sampling and hypothesis testing

Keep population-genetics slides alongside the practicals. In particular, HWE is a model expectation; identity by descent is defined on an ancestral base; and dosage alone does not identify phase.

Quantitative-genetics sequence

Practicals 1–5 follow the mouse analysis from observed traits to genetic evaluation. Later practicals use explicitly hypothetical examples to separate objectives, information, selection, inheritance and programme assessment.

Practical Main question Useful preparation
QG 1: Quantitative traits What variation is visible in the mouse records? Means, variance and basic R
QG 2: Single-locus variation How do additive and dominance effects contribute to variation? QG 1; PG 1–2
QG 3: Genetic parameters How do pedigree relationships support variance estimation? QG 1–2; linear models and matrix notation
QG 4: Pedigree breeding values How do BLUP, shrinkage and reliability relate? QG 3
QG 5: Genomic breeding values How do genomic information and held-out prediction enter evaluation? QG 3–4; PG 1
QG 6: Objectives and selection indices What is valued, and how should information be weighted? Covariance and linear models; QG 3–5 are helpful
QG 7: Selection policies and mating plans How do rules and reproductive contributions turn scores into a plan? QG 6
QG 8: Simulating selection response How do inheritance and a fixed reporting base reveal realized gain? QG 7; PG 1 and PG 4
QG 9: Relationships, mating and inbreeding How can equal contributions produce different offspring inbreeding? Relationship recursion from QG 3; PG 3; QG 7–8
QG 10: Validating predictions and decisions What do family splits, calibration and shortlist outcomes establish? Regression; QG 5; genetic/environmental distinction
QG 11: Comparing breeding programmes How do recording policies affect gain, diversity and resource use? QG 6–10; PG 4 and PG 6
QG 12: Genotype by environment and heritability How does replication across environments define genetic variance and entry-mean heritability? QG 2; variance and ANOVA; fits before QG 3 in the slide flow

Use the quantitative-genetics slides to connect trait variation, gene action, covariance, heritability and response to selection. Reading a predecessor can provide conceptual preparation without running its full code.

Topics needing additional worked practicals

Topic in the slides Current limit Suggested next exercise

For additional applications, the population-genetics slides discuss conservation, medicine, forensics and the molecular clock. PG 1–7 supply foundations for those topics, but do not provide dedicated applied practicals. Mutation–drift and mutation–selection balance also need their own worked synthesis if included in assessment.

The current synthetic programme examples use phenotype scores. Replacing them with an externally fitted evaluation would be a further extension, requiring an information schedule and validation target. QG 12 now covers genotype-by-environment interaction and broad-sense heritability; teach it after QG 2 and before QG 3 when following the slide flow.

PG 8 now supplies formal HWE inference. Teach it after PG 2–3 when the aim includes hypothesis testing, keeping observed departures separate from explanations of their cause.

Suggested teaching routes

Foundations of genetic variation

Read PG 1 → PG 2 → PG 3 → PG 4, then QG 1 → QG 2 → QG 12. Students move from allele coding and random-union expectations to sources of trait variation.

Checkpoint: explain why a heterozygosity deficit does not identify pedigree inbreeding, and why a locus’s dominance effect and average allele-substitution effect are different quantities.

Genetic evaluation and validation

Review PG 1–2, then work through QG 1 → QG 2 → QG 3 → QG 4 → QG 5 → QG 10. This route follows the mouse analysis and ends by examining what a validation split actually tests.

Checkpoint: distinguish predicted breeding values, phenotype predictions, model-based reliability and empirical validation. State which information is available at the prediction date and whether validation animals have relatives in training.

Population change and evolutionary selection

Work through PG 1 → PG 2 → PG 3 → PG 4 → PG 5 → PG 6 → PG 7. Use QG 2 as a bridge between gene action for a trait and genotype fitness.

Checkpoint: separate deterministic expectations from finite-population realizations. Fitness dominance in PG 7 is a different model quantity from phenotypic dominance in QG 2; economic weights in QG 6 describe another decision layer.

Breeding-programme design

Review PG 3–4 and PG 6, plus the evaluation concepts in QG 3–5. Then use QG 6 → QG 7 → QG 8 → QG 9 → QG 10 → QG 11. For a shorter design session, focus on QG 6, 7, 9 and 11, reading the simulation and validation assumptions before the capstone.

Checkpoint: define the objective, candidate eligibility, information schedule, parent contributions, mating, genetic base, calendar horizon and budget before comparing outcomes. Higher score correlation alone does not select a programme.

For QG 3–5, 9 and 11, retain the tutorials/ and scripts/ directory arrangement. The helper script supplies separate functions; loading it does not download data or fit a model until those functions are called. QG 9 and 11 use only its relationship recursion and do not require qgg.

Install qgg and gsim before the relevant session using the instructions in the practicals. The installed gsim must expose the breeding building blocks used in QG 7, 8 and 11. Data downloads in the mouse route need internet access. The website’s explanations and outputs can be read without installing R.

Course materials own the explanations and exercises. Package connections do not imply that every teaching calculation is an exported package function. Predictions, simulated truth and observed records remain distinct throughout the routes.

Instructor preparation and assessment

Before a session, choose the route and check its package and input requirements. Run the selected examples, then ask students to explain the assumptions before interpreting their numerical outputs. Use the collapsible solutions after discussion.

A short assessment can ask students to:

  1. Identify the counted allele, phenotype units, missing-data rule and population denominator.
  2. Explain the model behind a frequency expectation, covariance or relationship matrix.
  3. State the evaluation target and training information, including family connections.
  4. Describe how parental contributions and pairings affect offspring expectations.
  5. Report gain and diversity on fixed references while keeping costs and time explicit.

For the capstone, assess the programme specification and interpretation alongside the code. Three synthetic runs illustrate paired comparisons; they do not establish a dependable real-world policy ranking. A broader experiment needs an agreed population, model, replication design and scope.
